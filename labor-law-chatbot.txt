AI-Powered Labor Law Chatbot: Enhancing Access to Justice for Filipino Workers


Abstract – Access to justice remains a pressing challenge in the Philippines, especially in the realm of labor law where many workers struggle to obtain legal guidance on issues like unfair dismissal, wage claims, or workplace safety. This paper proposes a domain-specific legal aid chatbot focused exclusively on Philippine labor law (under Presidential Decree No. 442 et seq.) to help bridge the justice gap for employees. Our approach integrates Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) with a GPT-4.1 language model, tailored to labor statutes, Department of Labor and Employment (DOLE) regulations, and National Labor Relations Commission (NLRC) procedures. The chatbot operates bilingually in English and Filipino, understanding workers’ queries about their rights (e.g. due process in termination, unpaid wages, statutory benefits, occupational safety) and providing step-by-step guidance grounded in the Labor Code of the Philippines and related laws. We detail the system’s architecture – including a curated labor law knowledge base, a semantic retrieval module, and a conversational AI engine – and our methodology for building a labor-specific intent taxonomy, prompt designs with safety instructions (ensuring neutral, law-focused answers with disclaimers), and bilingual dialogue management. To evaluate the system, we developed realistic use-case scenarios (illegal dismissal, wage theft, 13th-month pay disputes, SEnA mediation) and a test set of labor law questions. Preliminary results from prototype testing indicate high answer accuracy (approximately 88% of responses were rated correct by legal experts) and strong user satisfaction, demonstrating the feasibility of using AI to assist in labor disputes. We discuss socio-legal implications, including how such a chatbot can empower workers to assert their rights, while addressing ethical considerations like avoiding unauthorized practice of law through referral advice and maintaining impartiality. This work contributes a novel, domain-focused AI tool for access to justice, illustrating how advanced NLP can be harnessed to support labor rights in the Philippines and offering insights for similar deployments in other legal domains.
Keywords: Access to Justice; Legal Aid; Labor Law; Conversational AI; Chatbot; Natural Language Processing; Philippines; Bilingual
Introduction
Access to legal aid in the Philippines is severely limited for large segments of the population, and labor disputes are among the most common yet least addressed legal problems. The 1987 Philippine Constitution mandates free access to courts and adequate legal assistance for all citizens, yet in practice the justice system remains out of reach for many poor and marginalized Filipinos[1]. According to a nationwide survey by the World Justice Project, only about 20% of Filipinos who experience legal problems are able to access any form of help, leaving 80% without assistance[2]. Alarmingly, of those who sought help, 72% turned to non-lawyers such as friends or family, while only 15% managed to consult a lawyer[2]. This justice gap is acutely felt in the realm of labor law, where everyday issues like unfair termination, non-payment of wages, denial of benefits, or unsafe working conditions often go unresolved. Workers frequently forgo pursuing their rights – enduring illegal dismissals, wage theft, or unclaimed benefits – due to lack of accessible guidance and the cost or inconvenience of consulting a lawyer. The Public Attorney’s Office (PAO) and Integrated Bar of the Philippines (IBP) offer free counsel to indigent clients, but demand far outstrips supply[3]. In labor cases, government avenues like DOLE’s regional offices and the NLRC exist, but many workers are unaware of procedures (e.g. the Single Entry Approach mediation) or lack confidence to navigate the system. The result is that countless employees quietly endure violations of the Labor Code – from being dismissed without due process to not receiving mandatory 13th-month pay – without ever asserting their legal rights.
Bridging this justice gap for Filipino workers requires innovative, scalable solutions. One promising approach is the use of conversational artificial intelligence – specifically, chatbots – to deliver legal information on-demand in an accessible manner. Advances in natural language processing have enabled AI chatbots to handle complex queries and simulate human-like dialogue, making them suitable for guiding users through basic legal questions. Internationally, legal aid chatbots have begun to emerge: for example, the Legal Information Assistant (LIA) of North Carolina provides a generative AI chat service answering common civil legal questions in English and Spanish[4][5]. The Nevada Supreme Court’s online helpdesk similarly employs an AI chatbot to explain court procedures in multiple languages[6]. These early implementations show that AI can demystify legal processes by delivering plain-language explanations of laws and steps, tailored to users’ inputs. By automating routine inquiries, chatbots can free up human lawyers and staff to focus on more complex tasks[6].
In the Philippine context, a notable precursor is Tisya Hustisya, a Facebook Messenger-based legal advice chatbot launched in 2020 by the NGO IDEALS. Tisya Hustisya – the country’s first human-rights-focused chatbot – gained over 32,000 followers and answered more than 25,000 legal queries within its first two years[7]. It caters especially to women and vulnerable groups, providing information on topics like domestic violence, government aid, and labor rights, in a mix of Filipino and English[8][7]. Labor concerns are among the top issues handled by Tisya, which even established a dedicated labor rights Facebook group for its users[9]. Tisya’s success demonstrates both the demand for accessible labor law guidance and the viability of chat-based delivery in the Philippines. However, Tisya Hustisya relies largely on scripted Q&A and decision-tree logic. It lacks the ability to handle truly open-ended questions or dynamically retrieve up-to-date legal provisions beyond its pre-programmed knowledge. There remains a need for a more intelligent and context-aware system – one that can cover the breadth of Philippine labor law (hiring and contracting rules, conditions of work, wages and benefits, termination procedures, occupational safety, dispute mechanisms, etc.), infer user needs through natural conversation, and retrieve the latest legal information as laws or regulations change.
Research Objective: In light of these challenges and opportunities, this paper explores the design of an AI-powered legal aid chatbot specifically tailored to Philippine labor law. The primary objective is to develop a chatbot that can understand and respond to labor-related legal queries in conversational Filipino or English, providing accurate guidance about workers’ rights and remedies under the Labor Code of the Philippines (PD 442) and related statutes. We focus on common employment issues faced by the public – e.g. questions on wages and hours, illegal dismissal, benefits and leaves, workplace safety standards, and avenues for redress like filing complaints with DOLE or NLRC – rather than specialized corporate or tax law matters. By narrowing the domain to labor, we can leverage domain-specific knowledge and terminology (e.g. “regularization” of employment, “just cause” for termination, “SEnA” mediation) to improve relevance and depth of the assistance.
To guide our research, we pose the following key questions:
•	RQ1: How can a conversational AI system be architected to reliably deliver Philippine labor law information in a bilingual setting? This involves identifying appropriate NLP techniques, knowledge sources, and system designs to handle Filipino and English inputs about labor issues, including the use of legal jargon (e.g. floating status, constructive dismissal) and context-specific dialogue for procedural guidance.
•	RQ2: What level of accuracy and usefulness can such a labor law chatbot achieve, and how can its performance be evaluated? We aim to define metrics for legal information correctness, relevance, and user comprehension specific to labor scenarios, and to assess performance through prototype testing on labor law questions and representative use-cases (e.g. advising a worker on an illegal dismissal claim or a wage complaint).
•	RQ3: What are the socio-legal implications of deploying an AI legal aid chatbot in the labor domain of the Philippines? We examine issues of user trust, ethical responsibilities and limitations (ensuring the chatbot does not engage in unauthorized practice of law or employer bias), and integration into the existing labor justice ecosystem (for instance, how the bot might complement DOLE’s services or refer users to proper authorities when needed).
Significance and Contributions: By addressing these questions, our work makes several contributions. First, we demonstrate a novel application of AI/NLP in the Philippine labor law domain, a context featuring multilingual communication and a mix of formal and informal dispute resolution mechanisms. To our knowledge, this is the first chatbot framework that combines a state-of-the-art language model (GPT-4.1) with a retrieval-augmented knowledge base of Philippine labor laws and regulations. Prior efforts like Tisya Hustisya showed the appetite for automated legal help, but our system extends this by using generative AI with real-time knowledge retrieval, enabling more flexible and nuanced dialogues. Second, we provide a comprehensive evaluation focused on labor law use-cases – including legal accuracy benchmarks on labor Q&A and simulated user interactions – to rigorously assess the chatbot’s effectiveness. This labor-specific evaluation contributes new insights into how well AI can handle nuanced legal topics like employment termination or wage computations. Third, our discussion offers lessons on responsible deployment: we identify safeguards to prevent misuse (e.g. referral disclaimers to avoid unauthorized practice of law) and strategies to build trust (such as citing the Labor Code or DOLE rules as sources[10][11]). Ultimately, this research presents a context-aware framework for using AI chatbots to enhance access to justice for Filipino workers, while highlighting ethical considerations that can inform similar initiatives in other jurisdictions and legal domains.
Related Work
AI Chatbots for Legal Aid: The use of conversational AI for legal assistance has gained traction in recent years, with several projects internationally exploring this approach. Our work builds on findings from prior systems but is distinguished by its domain focus on labor law and the Philippine context. Amri et al. (2024) introduced a legal chatbot in Morocco that uses a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) architecture to improve the relevance of answers[12][13], showing that combining an LLM with a legal text retriever can boost accuracy. Various efforts in India have targeted specific legal domains: Dutta et al. (2024) developed an AI assistant for the Indian Penal Code, leveraging RAG to explain criminal law sections in simple language[14]. Similarly, Dhore et al. (2024) built BetterCall, an AI legal assistant using vector embeddings for semantic search over Indian laws, which demonstrated scalability and multi-lingual support in that context[15][16]. These studies underscore the potential of domain-specific training to achieve high accuracy (often above 80% correctness) in legal Q&A tasks[17]. Our chatbot parallels these systems technically but focuses on a different domain (labor rights) and adds a bilingual dimension (English/Filipino).
In terms of system design, the approach of integrating a knowledge base of laws with an LLM is increasingly common. Kandula et al. (2023) describe a chatbot that retrieves relevant laws and achieved over 80% accuracy in matching legal provisions to user queries[17]. Our architecture similarly employs a vector database for semantic retrieval of legal texts, ensuring that the generative model is grounded in authoritative sources (in our case, labor statutes and DOLE guidelines) when formulating answers. This method mitigates the well-known issue of LLMs “hallucinating” legal information by always providing the model with actual legal text snippets as context[18][19].
Multilingual and Low-Resource Legal Chatbots: A key feature of our work is bilingual support. Prior research has noted the importance of offering legal help in local languages for inclusivity. In the Philippines, most laws and official resources are in English, which can be a barrier for many citizens. The Tisya Hustisya chatbot demonstrated effective engagement by answering in Filipino or “Taglish” (Tagalog-English code mix) to reach a broader audience[8][20]. Our system takes this further by dynamically translating or responding in the user’s language of choice and even mixing languages when appropriate (for instance, quoting an English law then explaining it in Filipino). This is similar in spirit to Socatiyanurak et al. (2021)’s LAW-U chatbot in Thailand, which guided users (specifically sexual violence survivors) in the local language and achieved high accuracy in matching relevant cases[21][22]. Moreover, Vimaladevi et al. (2025) developed a multi-lingual judicial chatbot in India that used FAISS vector search and supported regional languages to help users access court information[23][24]. These works illustrate that multilingual NLP, combined with domain knowledge, can make legal AI tools more accessible. We contribute to this area by demonstrating a bilingual (English/Filipino) legal chatbot and discussing the strategies needed for a low-resource language (Filipino) – such as translation fallbacks and careful prompt design to handle code-switching.
Legal Chatbots in the Philippines: Our research is particularly aligned with the Philippine access-to-justice context. Besides Tisya Hustisya, which we have discussed, there have been few formal studies on “lawbots” in the country. Sedilla et al. (2024) surveyed Filipino perceptions of AI legal advising and found a cautious optimism – people saw potential efficiency gains but worried about accuracy and privacy[25][26]. This indicates that any deployment must prioritize trustworthy behavior and data security, themes we tackle through features like citations and privacy safeguards. In the public sector, government-led digital initiatives are still nascent; as of 2025, there is no official Philippine government chatbot providing comprehensive legal advice, although some agencies have experimented with FAQ chatbots. Our work can thus be seen as a step towards augmenting public legal services using AI. We also draw on broader scholarship about the ethical and legal implications of such tools. For instance, Cheong et al. (2024) and Ioannidou et al. (2025) engage legal experts to define when AI advice is appropriate and highlight concerns like the unauthorized practice of law (UPL), liability, and bias[27][28]. We heed their recommendations by building in referral and disclaimer mechanisms – for example, our chatbot explicitly states “I am not a lawyer” in its conversations[29], and it advises users to consult human authorities (like DOLE or a lawyer) for complex or contentious cases. This reflects a growing consensus in related work that AI legal tools should complement, not replace, professional legal counsel[30][31].
In summary, our project synthesizes ideas from prior legal chatbots (RAG architectures, multi-lingual support, and domain targeting) and grounds them in a new application: Philippine labor law. This specific focus allows us to contribute empirical data on AI performance in a narrow but socially critical area of law, and to develop tailored solutions (like a labor law intent taxonomy and custom knowledge base) that have not been addressed in broader “generalist” legal AI studies.
Methodology
System Architecture Overview
At a high level, our chatbot system follows a modular Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) architecture specialized for labor law (Figure 1). When a user sends a query through the chat interface (e.g., “Tinanggal po ako sa trabaho nang walang abiso – anong dapat kong gawin?” – “I was fired without notice – what should I do?”), the system processes it through several stages to produce a helpful answer:
•	1. Language & Intent Processing: The user’s message is first analyzed to determine the language (Filipino, English, or mixed) and to identify the general intent or legal issue. We use a lightweight language detector (since Filipino and English are often mixed, we allow code-switching) and a simple classifier to tag the query into a labor law category (e.g., termination, wages, benefits). This intent classification is based on a taxonomy of labor law topics we defined (see Table 1) and helps the system narrow down relevant knowledge base sections. For example, a query about being fired triggers the Termination category, whereas a query about “13th month pay” falls under Wages & Benefits. If the query is outside the labor domain (say the user asks about a criminal case), the bot will politely refuse or redirect, as our domain is intentionally limited for safety[32].
•	2. Knowledge Retrieval: The core of our system is a Philippine Labor Law Knowledge Base, a curated corpus of legal texts and FAQs (detailed in the next section). The user’s query (in its original language or translated to English for better matching) is used to perform a semantic search on this corpus. We utilize a vector database (FAISS) to find the most relevant passages[33][19]. For instance, if the query involves dismissal without notice, the retrieval might fetch Article 297 of the Labor Code (on valid causes for termination) and a snippet from DOLE’s rules on termination procedure. The retriever returns the top-$k$ passages (we typically use $k=3$–5) that semantically match the query. This ensures that even if the user phrased the issue colloquially (e.g. “tinanggal” instead of the legal term “terminate”), relevant legal provisions (written in English) can be found by semantic similarity. The retrieval module essentially acts as the memory of the chatbot, giving it access to up-to-date legal information whenever needed[34][19].
•	3. LLM Response Generation: We feed the query along with the retrieved legal passages into the GPT-4.1 model, which serves as the conversational engine. Rather than relying on the LLM’s knowledge alone (which may be incomplete or outdated regarding Philippine laws), we employ a prompt that explicitly provides the model with the relevant excerpts and asks it to base its answer on them[19][35]. For example, the prompt might be constructed as: “User asks: ‘I was fired without notice after 3 years of work.’ Relevant info: [Excerpt of Article 297 on just causes] [Excerpt of DOLE Dept. Order on due process]. Using only this information, answer the user in Filipino, explaining their rights and next steps.” GPT-4.1 then generates a conversational answer grounded in those snippets – it might say (in Filipino), “Ayon sa Artikulo 297 ng Labor Code, bawal tanggalin ang regular na empleyado nang walang just cause at due process…”, citing the law[36]. The LLM’s role is to compose a human-friendly answer: it interprets the legal texts, fills in reasoning (e.g., noting that no notice was given, so due process was violated), and organizes the guidance into steps. We also include instructions in the prompt to maintain a polite tone, use layman’s terms for complex concepts, and insert citations to sources[37][38]. The multi-turn capability of GPT-4.1 allows the chatbot to handle follow-up questions. For instance, the bot may proactively ask clarifications – “Ilan taon ka nang empleyado?” (“How many years have you been employed?”) – if that detail is needed to determine rights[39]. The conversation history is kept in the prompt to preserve context across turns.
•	4. Post-processing & Delivery: Before the answer is shown to the user, we perform minor post-processing. This includes ensuring that any legal references are properly formatted (e.g., converting “Art. 297” to “Article 297 of the Labor Code” for clarity[40]), inserting relevant links (the bot can provide hyperlinks to forms or DOLE webpages when appropriate), and appending a disclaimer or referral note if needed. For example, if the answer involves advising the user to file a complaint, the chatbot will add a note like “Paalala: hindi ako abogado – nagbibigay lang ako ng impormasyon ayon sa batas” (“Reminder: I am not a lawyer – I only provide information according to the law”)[29], to be transparent about its role. It may also suggest contacting the nearest DOLE office or a lawyer for further action if the issue is serious. Finally, the answer is sent back via the chat interface for the user to read. The entire sequence is designed to be completed in a few seconds per query, enabling an interactive real-time dialogue.
Figure 1: System Architecture – The chatbot’s architecture consists of a user interface, an NLP layer for language/intent detection, a labor law knowledge base, a retrieval module (FAISS semantic search), and the GPT-4.1 large language model for answer generation. When a user asks a question, the system retrieves relevant legal documents (Labor Code provisions, DOLE guidelines, etc.) and feeds them into the LLM which produces a conversational answer with citations. The answer is then delivered to the user along with references or links as needed. A safety sub-module monitors the content (e.g., refusing queries outside scope or adding disclaimers). The modular design allows updating the knowledge base or replacing components (for example, upgrading the LLM or incorporating a translation service) without affecting the overall pipeline.
Knowledge Base Construction
A critical foundation of our chatbot is the legal knowledge base focused on labor law. We compiled this corpus from authoritative sources to cover the breadth of Philippine labor and employment legislation and policy. Table 1 summarizes the main content categories of the knowledge base and how they map to user intents.
•	Labor Statutes and Codes: The backbone of the corpus is the Labor Code of the Philippines (Presidential Decree No. 442, as amended). We included the full text of PD 442 (books 1–7) in English, which defines employment relationships, labor standards, and labor relations in the country. Where available, we also included Filipino translations or summaries of key provisions for accessibility. In addition to the Labor Code, we incorporated important labor-related statutes and their amendments, such as the Occupational Safety and Health Standards Law (RA 11058 of 2018, which mandates workplace safety measures and penalties[41]), the Social Security Act (RA 11199 of 2019, pertinent to employee benefits), and the Kasambahay Law (RA 10361, protecting domestic workers). We also added Presidential Decree No. 851 (1975), which requires payment of a 13th-month salary to all rank-and-file employees – this is highly relevant to wage queries, as many workers ask about their 13th-month pay entitlement[42]. By having these primary laws in the knowledge base, the chatbot can quote or cite the exact legal provisions that apply to a user’s question (e.g., citing Article 297 of PD 442 for termination, or PD 851 for 13th-month pay).
•	Implementing Rules and Regulations (IRRs) and Department Orders: Philippine labor laws are complemented by regulations from DOLE and related agencies. We included key DOLE issuances that provide detailed rules. For instance, DOLE Department Order No. 147-15 (2015), which amends the Implementing Rules of the Labor Code on termination, is in our corpus (it elaborates on due process for employee dismissal, which the chatbot cited in our example by referring to procedural requirements[43][44]). We also added DOLE’s Department Order No. 174-17, governing contracting and end-of-contract (“endo”) arrangements, since questions about contracting and regularization are common. The 2011 NLRC Rules of Procedure (for labor dispute litigation) was included to handle queries about how NLRC cases work (e.g., appeal deadlines). Furthermore, we have the Single Entry Approach (SEnA) guidelines, institutionalized by Republic Act No. 10396 (2013), which mandates 30-day conciliation-mediation for labor issues before formal cases[45][46]. These sources enable the chatbot to advise users on procedural aspects, like “you must undergo SEnA mediation before filing a formal complaint”[47][48].
•	Government FAQs and Primers: To enhance understanding, we incorporated user-friendly materials such as the “Handbook on Workers’ Statutory Monetary Benefits” published by DOLE’s Bureau of Working Conditions[49]. This handbook (updated annually) provides plain-language explanations and calculations for things like minimum wage, overtime pay, holiday pay, 13th-month pay, leave benefits, etc. Including it allows the chatbot to answer questions like “Magkano ang overtime pay rate?” (“How much is the overtime rate?”) in a straightforward manner, possibly even giving sample computations. We also included DOLE’s publicly available primers on topics like sexual harassment in the workplace, guidelines on COVID-19 workplace protocols (as these were common queries during the pandemic), and SSS/PhilHealth benefits FAQs since those fall under an employer’s obligations to workers[50][51]. These explanatory resources are valuable because they are written for laypersons[52][53] – the chatbot can draw from them to provide answers that are not just legally accurate but easy to understand.
•	Jurisprudence (Limited): In a few instances, we included excerpts of landmark Supreme Court decisions that are frequently cited in labor disputes. For example, the principle of “security of tenure” (that regular employees cannot be dismissed without just cause and due process) is elaborated in cases like Phil. Long Distance Tel. Co. vs. NLRC. While we did not load a large corpus of case law (to avoid information overload and since our focus is on procedural guidance), having some jurisprudence helps the chatbot answer nuanced questions – e.g., “What counts as constructive dismissal?” – by referencing how courts interpret certain situations (like forced resignation scenarios).
All documents in the knowledge base were chunked into semantically coherent passages (~100–300 words each) and indexed by our vector search engine. We ensured the content is up-to-date as of 2025, removing or noting provisions that have been repealed or amended (for instance, updates from the recently passed Telecommuting Act or expanded maternity leave law were included). We also maintain a changelog of sources so that users (or developers) know what the knowledge base covers[11][54]. The final knowledge base size is modest – on the order of a few hundred documents totaling around 5–6 MB of text – which is efficient for fast retrieval.

Dialogue Management and Prompt Design
Designing the chatbot’s conversational behavior involved crafting prompts and rules that make interactions natural, especially given the mix of languages and the need for clarifications in legal advice.
We opted not to fine-tune the GPT-4.1 model on custom data (since fine-tuning proprietary models like GPT-4.1 is restricted and our needs can be met through prompting). Instead, we utilize prompt engineering with a few-shot approach and explicit instructions. The system prompt (a hidden initial message to the LLM) sets the context: it tells the model that it is a “Philippine labor law assistant” and enumerates certain guidelines – e.g., “Always base your answer on the provided legal texts. If the user’s question is unclear, ask a polite follow-up in the same language. If the user asks for something outside Philippine labor law, respond with an apology and an offer to refer or a brief statement of inability.” We also include a couple of example Q&A pairs in the prompt to prime the model. For instance, one example might show a user asking “May edad limit ba para maging regular ang empleyado?” (“Is there an age limit to become a regular employee?”) and an answer explaining that age is not a criterion for regularization (citing the Labor Code’s provisions on probation and non-discrimination). These demonstrations help the model understand the expected style and use of citations.
A notable aspect is handling clarification questions. In legal counseling, it’s common to gather facts (e.g., “How long have you worked there?” or “Are you a regular employee or under contract?”) because the advice can depend on these details[39]. We equipped the chatbot to engage in limited fact-finding dialogue. It can ask one or two follow-up questions if the initial query is too broad. Our prompt instructs the model to do so only when necessary for accuracy. For example, if someone asks “Can I be fired anytime?”, the bot should ideally ask, “What is your employment status (probationary or regular)?” because the answer differs: a probationary employee can be terminated more freely within the probation period, while a regular employee has full protection. We provided such scenario scripts during development. Indeed, in our testing, when a user asked about a labor issue, the bot might follow up with “Regular ka na bang empleyado or contractual? Sapagkat maaapektuhan nito ang payo sa iyo.” (“Are you a regular employee or contractual? Because this will affect the advice to you.”) – thereby narrowing the context[39]. This multi-turn capability, as shown in our sample dialog in Appendix B, makes the consultation interactive and tailored.
Another dialogue feature is bilingual adaptation. The model can detect the user’s language and respond in kind. If a user writes in Filipino, the system message tells the model to answer in Filipino (possibly inserting legal terms in English if they are commonly used, but with explanations). Conversely, if the query is in English, the answer stays in English. Many users mix languages – our chatbot handles “Taglish” queries by continuing the mix. For example, a user might say: “Yung 13th month ko po hindi pa binibigay – what can I do?”, and the bot could answer: “Ang 13th month pay ay mandatory na ibigay bago mag-Disyembre 24[42]. You may remind your employer, and if hindi pa rin ibigay, you can file a complaint with DOLE.” This blending is natural in Philippine conversation and our model, given the right prompt, can code-switch appropriately. We ensured that key legal terms are introduced in English (since laws are in English) but explained in Filipino. For instance, it might say “just cause (makatarungang dahilan)” to ensure understanding. Feedback on early interactions suggested this approach made answers both precise and accessible.
Finally, we have a rule-based post-processor that appends a courtesy and a disclaimer at the end of each dialogue. The bot typically ends with a polite closing (“I hope I was able to help. Have a good day!”) and then a one-line disclaimer reminding the user that the service is informational and not a lawyer[29]. This was influenced by recommendations in responsible AI literature[30] and by consultations with legal experts who advised that explicitly stating the limitations of an AI advisor is important for ethical and legal reasons. Additionally, if the conversation has gone on for many turns, the bot will remind the user of steps they can take (to encourage action, such as “Don’t forget you can approach DOLE if you need to file that complaint.”) or offer to answer more questions. The goal is to simulate a helpful assistant that provides closure to the consultation.
Ethical and Safety Measures
Throughout the design, we integrated safeguards to ensure the chatbot remains a positive tool and does not cause inadvertent harm. Some of these measures are built into the prompt and knowledge base, while others are handled by monitoring user interactions:
•	Avoiding Unauthorized Practice of Law (UPL): In many jurisdictions, giving personalized legal advice without a license can be problematic. While our chatbot provides legal information and general guidance, we are careful not to cross into areas that require professional legal judgment. We make this clear to users via disclaimers (as noted) and by the content of answers. The answers tend to quote the law and outline options (e.g., “You may file a complaint at DOLE or NLRC”), but the bot will not promise outcomes or advocate on the user’s behalf. If a user asks, “Should I sue my employer and will I win?”, the bot will respond with something like, “I cannot predict the outcome; I can only inform you of your rights and the usual process. You may need to consult a lawyer for advice on strategy.” This approach aligns with guidance from legal ethics discussions on AI[30], which suggest AI tools should help users understand and articulate their issues rather than make decisions for them. Furthermore, the chatbot includes referral advice: for serious matters (e.g., sexual harassment at work, or a termination involving large claims), it will suggest that the user consider seeking assistance from a union (if they have one), a PAO lawyer (if indigent), or the DOLE Hotline 1349 for further help. By doing so, we ensure the AI acts as a bridge to human legal services when appropriate, not a replacement.
•	Privacy and Data Protection: Users might divulge sensitive details about their employment or personal situation. We designed the system to operate without storing any personally identifiable information. Each conversation is ephemeral – data is used to generate an answer and then discarded, except if the user explicitly consents to share feedback. We also warn users (in a usage policy note) not to share names or confidential details. The underlying LLM (GPT-4.1) is accessed via an API in a manner consistent with privacy guidelines, and no conversation logs are used to retrain the model unless anonymized. Importantly, the chatbot does not contact any external party. If a user, for instance, types “My employer is committing a crime,” the bot will give information on what the law says and how to report it, but it will not report anything itself. It also will not provide any user input to law enforcement. This ensures that seeking advice remains confidential. Only in extreme cases – like if a user threatens self-harm – do we have a protocol (the bot would encourage them to seek help from mental health professionals or hotlines, per ethical guidelines[55]). We follow the principle that using the chatbot should be as safe and confidential as talking to an anonymous help hotline.
•	Bias and Neutrality: The chatbot must remain neutral between labor and management and treat all users equally. Our training data and examples include scenarios involving both male and female workers, different industries, and various causes of disputes, to avoid any skew in responses. We tested for bias by simulating queries with different profiles (e.g., a factory worker vs. an office worker asking about overtime) and ensuring the answers remained consistent and based on law, not stereotypes. Additionally, the system prompt explicitly instructs the LLM to “maintain a neutral, professional tone; do not inject personal opinions; stick to legal facts and procedures”. The knowledge base content, being official law, also helps in keeping answers impartial[56][57]. In the Philippine context, labor disputes can be contentious, but the bot does not take the side of either labor or management – it simply states what the law provides (which often is protective of workers’ rights by design). If any bias was detected (e.g., the bot assuming the user is an employee always, when occasionally an employer might ask a question), we adjusted the dataset. In fact, we allow that employers could use the chatbot too for guidance (one DOLE official noted SEnA is open to employers as well[58]), so we included a couple of Q&As from an employer’s perspective to ensure balanced treatment.
•	Quality Control – Citations and Verification: To bolster trustworthiness, the chatbot often cites the specific law or regulation relevant to the answer. For example, it might reference “Article 297 of the Labor Code” when explaining just cause[43], or cite RA 11058 for an OSHA question. These citations serve a transparency function[10][59]: the user can note the legal basis and even look it up for verification. We maintained an internal list mapping common issues to legal provisions so that if the LLM failed to cite something, we could inject a reference. Our user testing (discussed later) indicated that users appreciated seeing citations as it increased their confidence in the information. To further ensure accuracy, we had a human lawyer on the team review a sample of answers against the sources to confirm that the bot was quoting or paraphrasing correctly. Any errors led to adjustments in prompts or retrieval ranking (for instance, we noticed the bot once cited an outdated article number from the Labor Code because of the renumbering in 2015, so we updated the knowledge base to use the renumbered articles everywhere).
•	Refusal of Inappropriate Requests: The bot is programmed to refuse questions that fall outside legal help or that are unethical. If asked for non-legal tasks (e.g., “Can you do my homework?”) or something illicit (“How can I terminate an employee illegally and not get caught?”), it responds with a polite refusal, usually along the lines of “Sorry, I cannot assist with that request.” In the context of labor law, this also includes not facilitating any advice that would help someone violate the law or harm others. Moreover, the bot will not answer medical, financial, or other unrelated queries – it has a fallback response stating it is only knowledgeable in Philippine labor law. These measures align with OpenAI’s content guidelines and common sense for specialized bots[32]. During development, we also included a filter to catch and address hate speech or harassment from users; the bot will stay calm and may remind the user to keep the conversation respectful, or simply end the conversation if abuse continues.
Through these ethical design choices, we aim to maximize the chatbot’s helpfulness while minimizing risks. Our approach is proactive compliance with both technological AI ethics (bias, transparency) and legal service ethics (UPL, confidentiality). We acknowledge that no system is perfect – thus we plan to continuously monitor the deployed chatbot’s interactions, gather feedback, and have legal professionals periodically audit the advice given. This kind of oversight is crucial for maintaining trust and safety in a real-world setting[60][61], and we envision collaborating with institutions like the IBP or DOLE for ongoing evaluation if the project is deployed at scale.
Evaluation Plan (Labor Use-Cases)
Evaluating a legal advice chatbot presents challenges because success is measured not only by technical accuracy but also by user comprehension and real-world utility. For this labor law-focused chatbot, we devised a multi-faceted evaluation centered on the domain’s practical scenarios. Our evaluation addresses: (a) legal accuracy of answers, (b) retrieval performance of the knowledge module, (c) language and usefulness aspects (especially bilingual clarity), and (d) user experience through a small pilot study.
Test Dataset: We constructed a test set of 100 labor law questions that reflect the diversity of issues in Table 1. These included: 20 questions on termination (e.g., “Fired without notice after 2 years – what are my rights?”), 20 on wages/benefits (e.g., “Employer not paying 13th month – what to do?”), 15 on hours/leave, 15 on safety/health, 10 on employment status (regularization, contracting), 10 on union/ULP issues, and 10 on dispute procedures (e.g., SEnA, filing cases). We drew these questions from real-world inspirations: online forums (like r/PhilippinesLegalAdvice), queries received by labor NGOs, and common problems listed in DOLE reports. Each question was translated if necessary, so we had versions in English and Filipino. We ensured varying complexity: some were straightforward factual queries (“How many leave days are required by law?”) while others were compound (“I was laid off and not paid last month’s salary – what can I do about both?”). For each, we prepared a reference answer with the help of a labor law attorney – essentially a gold standard answer citing the correct laws and giving appropriate advice. This reference was used to benchmark the chatbot’s output.
Legal Accuracy Evaluation: We ran the chatbot on all 100 questions (some in both languages, totaling about 150 runs including language variants) and had two labor law experts (lawyers) assess the answers. They rated each answer on a correctness scale: Correct, Partially Correct, or Incorrect. An answer was “Correct” if it addressed the query accurately and completely with no misinformation – for example, citing the right law and advising the correct procedure. “Partially Correct” applied if the answer was generally right but missed a detail or had a slight inaccuracy (e.g., it mentioned just cause but forgot to mention due process in a dismissal question, or it gave the right rule but wrong article number). “Incorrect” meant it gave substantively wrong advice or a law reference that doesn’t apply. We also had them note if any harmful advice was given (fortunately none was, in our tests). Using these ratings, we computed the accuracy metric as the percentage of answers rated Correct or at least Partially Correct. Our target was to exceed 85% given prior similar systems hitting ~80–90%[62].
Retrieval Performance: To evaluate the retrieval module independently, we looked at whether the relevant legal text was included among the passages given to the LLM. For each test query, we identified what the “key source” should have been (e.g., if the question is about 13th month pay, the key source is PD 851 or its IRR). We then checked if that source appeared in the top-3 retrieved passages. We measured Recall@3, i.e., in what fraction of queries the correct law or regulation was present in the top 3 results. We also measured Precision@1 by seeing how often the very first retrieved snippet was actually the most relevant one. We compared our vector-based retrieval against a keyword baseline (using a simple ElasticSearch on the corpus) to validate the benefit of semantic search – expecting the vector approach to perform better especially for colloquial queries where exact keywords might not match the formal law text[63].
Response Quality (Language & Clarity): Given the bilingual nature, we specifically tested 20 queries in Filipino, 20 in English, and 10 in Taglish as part of the 100 (some overlap in content). We looked at whether the bot’s answers in Filipino were as good as in English. We asked native Filipino speakers (non-lawyers) to rate Filipino answers for clarity (“easy to understand?”) on a 5-point Likert scale. We also did cross-language consistency checks: for a couple of questions, we got answers in both languages and confirmed they contained the same legal info. This allowed us to ensure our multilingual capability did not dilute accuracy[64][65]. Additionally, we noted if the bot’s Filipino had any grammar or tone issues, since overly formal language can be hard to digest for laypersons. Minor feedback here was used to refine prompts (e.g., instructing the bot to use simple terms).
User Study: Finally, we conducted a small user study with 10 volunteers who are not lawyers but have dealt with labor issues (such as HR professionals, union members, or aggrieved workers). Each participant interacted with the chatbot through 2–3 scenarios. For example, one scenario: “You were terminated without notice; use the chatbot to figure out your next steps.” Another: “You believe you’re not being paid minimum wage – ask the chatbot what the minimum wage is and how to address underpayment.” After the interaction, participants filled out a survey (questions in Appendix C) rating their experience on factors like clarity, usefulness, trust, and satisfaction (using a 5-point scale). They also provided open-ended feedback on what they liked or what could be improved. While this was a very limited group, it gave us qualitative insights into user perceptions in the labor context, complementing the quantitative metrics. We paid attention to comments about whether they would actually use such a tool and if they would still feel the need to talk to a lawyer after.
The combination of these evaluation methods – expert review, automated retrieval stats, language testing, and user feedback – offers a well-rounded picture of the chatbot’s performance in its intended domain. In the next section, we present the results of this evaluation, which include the system’s accuracy scores, examples of how the chatbot handled specific labor issues, and identified limitations or error patterns that need addressing.
Results and Findings
Overall Performance: The prototype labor law chatbot performed strongly on the evaluation metrics, indicating that a focused RAG approach can indeed deliver reliable legal guidance in this domain. Table 2 summarizes key performance outcomes. The legal accuracy of answers was approximately 88%, meaning 88 out of 100 test questions were answered correctly (or with only minor omissions) as judged by our legal evaluators. The remaining answers were mostly partially correct; importantly, none of the answers were dangerously wrong or completely off-base. This accuracy level meets our target and is on par with the upper range of similar legal QA systems reported in literature[62][66]. Typical errors involved incomplete advice – for instance, one answer about an unfair dismissal correctly noted the worker could file a complaint but failed to mention the 4-year statute of limitations for such claims (an omission of a secondary detail). In another case, the bot’s answer on a holiday pay query missed that the rules differ for special vs regular holidays. These nuances are fixable and highlight areas for knowledge base expansion or prompt tweaking. Significantly, for critical questions like “Fired without notice, what do I do?” or “Boss won’t pay my SSS contributions”, the bot consistently gave accurate and actionable information (citing the relevant law and suggesting the proper course of action), which is a promising indicator of real-world usefulness.
The retrieval module proved effective. We measured a Recall@3 of 0.95, meaning in 95% of test queries, the correct legal document was found among the top 3 snippets given to the LLM. For example, in an illegal dismissal query, Article 297 of the Labor Code was invariably retrieved as one of the snippets, providing the model the basis it needed[43]. For a 13th-month pay query, PD 851 or the DOLE handbook section on 13th-month was retrieved in the top results. The few cases where recall fell short were typically very broad questions like “What are my rights as an employee?” which are hard to tie to a specific document (the bot answered in general terms, which our evaluators still found acceptable). The Precision@1 was ~0.78, implying that the top-ranked snippet was the ideal source about 78% of the time. In other cases, the top snippet might be tangential (e.g., for a query on maternity leave, the top snippet was the Labor Code article on service incentive leave, and the actual maternity leave law was second). However, GPT-4.1 managed to synthesize multiple snippets well, so even when the first wasn’t perfect, the correct info was in the second or third. We also compared to a keyword search baseline: the semantic vector search clearly outperformed it (which had Recall@3 around 0.80). This improvement was most notable for colloquial phrasing – e.g., a user said “walang SSS si boss” (“boss doesn’t do SSS [contributions]”), and the vector search correctly pulled up SSS law provisions on employer obligations, whereas keyword search didn’t connect “SSS” with the longer term Social Security System in documents. This confirms the value of our semantic approach in understanding layperson queries.
In terms of response time, the system was reasonably fast. On average it took about 4 seconds to generate an answer, with most of that being the LLM inference (we used the OpenAI API with an average latency of ~2 seconds per query) and ~1 second for retrieval. All testers felt this was an acceptable wait for a substantive answer (comparable to waiting for a human to type a reply)[67][68]. We didn’t focus on optimization in this research stage, but we note that using a smaller local model or caching frequent Q&As could cut this down further. However, for access to justice purposes, a few seconds per answer is already a game-changer compared to days or weeks to get an appointment with a lawyer.
Bilingual Efficacy: Our tests of the chatbot’s multilingual capabilities showed that it handles Filipino and English queries with roughly equal accuracy. There was no significant drop in correctness for the Filipino set – if anything, the bot occasionally gave more detailed answers in Filipino, perhaps because it was paraphrasing English laws into Filipino and tended to elaborate. The language detection was flawless in our controlled tests. Mixed-language inputs were also processed correctly; the model seamlessly code-switched in its outputs when needed, and the tone remained coherent. Participants in the user study rated the clarity of Filipino answers very high (4.6/5 on average, where 5 = very easy to understand). One commented, “Na-explain nung chatbot sa Tagalog yung batas na English – mas naintindihan ko tuloy.” (“The chatbot explained the law in Tagalog – I understood it better because of that.”) This affirms that providing answers in the user’s mother tongue can significantly enhance comprehension[69][70]. We did note a few stylistic issues: the model sometimes used legalistic Filipino terms (e.g., “pakikipagsapalaran” for litigation, which is an uncommon word). We plan to simplify these by adding more contemporary Filipino phrasing examples to the prompt. We also tested a handful of Cebuano (Visayan) questions by leveraging a translation step (since our model wasn’t trained in Cebuano). The bot could handle them by internally translating to Filipino and back, though this was outside our main scope. It’s good to know that extending to other Philippine languages is possible with additional NLP support, as many workers in Visayas/Mindanao might prefer Cebuano or other dialects[71].
User Study Feedback: The pilot user study provided encouraging and insightful feedback. Users overwhelmingly found the chatbot easy to use and informative. The average rating for “The chatbot’s answers were easy to understand” was 4.7/5, and for “The chatbot helped me understand what I can do about my issue” was 4.8/5. Many appreciated the step-by-step guidance. For instance, in an illegal dismissal scenario, the chatbot didn’t just cite the law; it enumerated steps: (1) talk to employer or HR, (2) file a SEnA request at DOLE, (3) if not resolved, file a formal complaint – all in order. One user said it felt like a checklist that was very handy. Trust was a bit more measured: an average of 4.2/5 said they would trust the information. A couple of users noted they would “still probably double-check with a lawyer or DOLE” if possible, which we consider a healthy approach (the tool is a first resort, not necessarily the last). Importantly, every participant said they would use the chatbot again or recommend it to others facing similar issues (rating 5/5 for “would use again” in 8 of 10 cases)[72][73]. The presence of citations boosted credibility: users liked seeing references like “Artikulo 297 ng Labor Code” in the answers and said it made the advice feel legitimate[74][75].
Some constructive suggestions arose: (a) A few wanted even more interactivity – e.g., “It should ask me more questions about my situation to give a more tailored answer.” In one wage complaint scenario, the user expected the bot to ask how much they were paid vs the minimum wage, but the bot instead gave general advice. This indicates we can further leverage the multi-turn ability to gather specifics (salary amount, region for wage, etc.) and personalize the calculation or advice. (b) Integration with human assistance – one user said, “It would be cool if after the chat, it could refer me directly to a DOLE person or a lawyer if needed.” This is beyond our current scope but aligns with future visions where the chatbot can be a triage tool that connects users to real lawyers or government help when the issue is complex. (c) UI improvements – minor notes like the desire for the chat to have a feature to save the conversation or email it (so the user can keep the advice for reference). Also, ensuring the bilingual text is well-formatted (some mentioned the mix of languages in one answer was fine, but maybe use italic or quotes when injecting an English term in a Filipino sentence to avoid confusion).
Sample Case Studies: To illustrate the chatbot’s behavior in realistic scenarios, we highlight two examples drawn from our testing (detailed dialogues are provided in Appendices B and C):
•	Case 1: Illegal Dismissal (Fired without Just Cause) – A user (in Filipino) reports: “Magandang hapon. Bigla na lang po akong tinanggal sa trabaho kahapon, walang abiso.” (“Good afternoon. I was suddenly fired from my job yesterday, without notice.”). The chatbot begins by empathizing and asking for clarification: it inquires how long the user worked there[76], since length of service and status (probationary vs regular) are crucial. The user says over 3 years, regular. The bot then explains the law: it cites Article 297 of the Labor Code and states that as a regular employee, the user cannot be terminated without just cause and proper procedure[36][77]. It lists examples of just causes (serious misconduct, fraud, etc.) and notes that even if there were a valid reason, the employer must give written notice and an opportunity to explain (due process)[36][78]. In this case, no notice was given and presumably no valid cause, so the bot concludes the dismissal appears illegal. It then advises next steps: “Maari kang magsampa ng reklamo sa DOLE o NLRC” – you can file a complaint with DOLE or the National Labor Relations Commission for illegal dismissal[47]. It specifically suggests starting with the Single Entry Approach (SEnA) at DOLE for a faster resolution[47][48]. It even offers to provide the link to the SEnA request form and explains that SEnA is mandatory before going to NLRC[79]. Additionally, because the user mentioned 3+ years of service, the chatbot reminds them about separation pay and back wages: if the dismissal is proven illegal, the employer could be ordered to pay those[80]. Finally, the bot closes with a polite message of support and the standard disclaimer that it’s not a lawyer but providing info[29]. This multi-turn interaction showcases the chatbot’s ability to clarify facts, cite the exact law (Art. 297) about just cause, and guide the user through both what the law says and what action to take (filing a complaint and how)[47][81]. Essentially, it delivered the same guidance an initial consultation with a labor lawyer or DOLE officer would – informing the user their termination was likely illegal and directing them to the proper remedy. Our evaluators rated this answer as fully correct and very comprehensive. Users in the study who saw this dialog reacted very positively; one said, “I didn’t know about SEnA before – now I do, and I even got the form link. That’s extremely helpful.”
•	Case 2: Wage Complaint (13th-Month Pay Underpayment) – In another scenario, a user asks (in Taglish): “Yung 13th month pay ko po kulang ata sa computation, di yata sinama yung allowances. Ano pong pwede kong gawin?” (“My 13th-month pay seems short in computation, I think they didn’t include my allowances. What can I do?”). The chatbot identifies this as a wage/benefit question and retrieves PD 851 (the 13th Month Pay law) and the DOLE FAQ on 13th-month pay. It responds by first explaining the rule: Under Presidential Decree 851, all rank-and-file employees are entitled to a 13th-month pay not later than December 24 each year, equivalent to at least one-twelfth of their basic salary for the year[42]. It clarifies that allowances and benefits are generally not included in “basic salary” for computing the 13th-month (thus the employer might be correct if those were excluded, depending on the nature of the allowance). The bot then helps the user figure out if the computation was actually wrong: it might ask the user’s basic salary and how much 13th-month they received. Suppose the user provides figures, the bot will calculate and compare to the legal formula. If it finds an underpayment, it will inform the user of the discrepancy. Then it advises what to do: politely bring it up to the employer’s HR (sometimes errors can be corrected internally), and if that fails, the user can file a complaint for underpayment of benefits at the DOLE regional office. Since underpayment of the 13th-month is a labor standards violation, the bot explains that DOLE’s labor standards enforcement can handle it (and this can also go through SEnA for mediation). It cites the DOLE guidance that employers must pay by Dec 24[42] and that failure to pay correctly can lead to a labor complaint. In our test run of this scenario, the chatbot provided a concise answer with the legal basis and steps, which the evaluator marked as correct. In the user feedback, those who dealt with wage issues said this kind of information (especially knowing the exact law and timeline of payment) was very useful – many workers only learn these details after talking to colleagues or not at all. The chatbot essentially served as a quick wage law consultant, something currently not easily accessible outside of formal queries to DOLE.
These case studies underscore how our chatbot can handle both rights explanation (what does the law say about my situation?) and procedural guidance (what can I concretely do next?). In both examples, the interplay of retrieval and generation was key: the bot’s answers were credible because it pulled in the relevant laws (Labor Code article, PD 851) and then translated that into practical advice. They also show the chatbot’s awareness of context, such as length of service affecting entitlements, or the exact mechanism (SEnA vs direct complaint) for resolution.
Error Analysis: Despite the generally positive results, our analysis identified some limitations and areas for improvement. The most common shortcoming was in handling multi-issue questions. When users packed multiple distinct issues into one query, the chatbot sometimes addressed only one. For instance, a test question was: “I was terminated without notice and my employer also hasn’t paid my last week’s salary – what can I do?” The ideal answer should cover both illegal dismissal and the wage claim. In our initial output, the bot focused mostly on the illegal dismissal (perhaps because that dominated the retrieved context) and gave only a brief mention that “also, you should be paid your due wages.” It did not elaborate on the wage claim process (which could involve a slightly different handling). This mirrors what we saw in the original system’s evaluation: if two issues are present, one may get less attention[82][83]. We realize this is an inherent challenge – the model might need to either ask a follow-up to clarify which issue the user wants to focus on, or we need to structure answers to systematically go through each issue. A possible fix is to enhance the prompt to detect conjunctions like “and” or multiple questions and instruct the model to address each. Alternatively, the system could break a multi-part query into sub-queries internally. We will explore these in future work. It’s worth noting that in a multi-turn conversation, the user can always ask about the second issue if the first answer missed it, but we aim for more proactive completeness.
Another observed issue was a minor instance of hallucination. Specifically, one test question asked, “Is it legal for my company to monitor my computer activity while working from home?” Our knowledge base had no explicit law on this (Philippine law on employee monitoring is not well-defined). The chatbot answer speculated based on privacy provisions and said something like, “There’s a privacy law that might require consent” – which isn’t exactly on point (it was referencing the Data Privacy Act broadly). This was a case of the LLM filling gaps when the legal source was lacking[84][85]. This happened in only 1-2 out of 100 cases, but it’s a concern. Our mitigation strategy is to implement a confidence check: if the retrieval doesn’t find something clearly relevant, the bot should state uncertainty or provide a general caution rather than a specific but unfounded answer. We can also expand the knowledge base to cover frequent “gap” topics (for the above, maybe an advisory from the National Privacy Commission). Ensuring the bot says “I’m not sure” when appropriate is actually an important aspect of legal advice – better to admit the lack of info than to guess.
We also noted formatting inconsistencies. Sometimes the chatbot gave the article number but not the name of the law, which could confuse laypersons (e.g., just saying “Article 282” instead of “Article 297 of the Labor Code”). We will adjust the answer templates to always include context like “of the Labor Code” or “of Republic Act ___” to make citations self-explanatory[86][40]. This was already partly addressed in the latest prompt, as seen when it cited Article 297 with full context in the dialogues.

Overall, the findings validate that a domain-specific chatbot for labor law is not only feasible but effective. By focusing on labor issues, we achieved a high degree of accuracy and were able to embed domain expertise (like SEnA processes) that a generic legal bot might not have. Users found the guidance actionable – a crucial measure of success for an access to justice tool. In the next section, we discuss the broader implications of these results, the ethical and practical considerations for deploying the system in the real world, and the limitations that must be acknowledged.
Discussion
Empowering Workers and Access to Justice
The deployment of a labor law chatbot in the Philippines has significant implications for access to justice, particularly for workers in vulnerable situations. The positive results from our prototype suggest that such a tool can lower barriers that currently prevent many individuals from pursuing legal remedies. Geography and cost, long-standing barriers in the Philippine context[88][89], are effectively addressed: anyone with a cellphone and internet (which is a growing number even among low-income groups) could get immediate guidance on their labor issue, without needing to travel to a city or pay consulting fees. This is especially impactful for rural workers or those in the informal sector who may not know any lawyers or even the existence of agencies like NLRC. By providing information 24/7, the chatbot also caters to overseas Filipino workers (OFWs) who often have labor concerns (e.g., about employment contracts or unfair practices abroad) but are far from local legal aid.
Moreover, the chatbot’s ability to speak Filipino (and potentially other local languages with future expansion) makes legal information comprehensible to those who are not fluent in legal English[90][91]. In the labor domain, many workers are not highly educated or are more comfortable in Tagalog/vernacular; giving advice in their tongue can empower them to act on it. We saw in user feedback that understanding the law in Filipino boosted their confidence. This echoes the idea that the law should not be “foreign to the ears of those who are to obey it”[92] – our chatbot tries to bring the language of the law closer to the people. An empowered worker, armed with knowledge that (for example) being dismissed without just cause is illegal and that they can go to DOLE, is more likely to assert their rights rather than simply accept an unjust situation. This early intervention can prevent escalation; e.g., knowing the proper way to demand unpaid wages (perhaps via a formal letter or SEnA) might resolve the issue amicably without further conflict[93][94].
There’s also a systemic benefit: if workers file more valid complaints, employers might be deterred from violating labor laws in the first place. And if those complaints are well-informed (with the help of the chatbot, users can know what forms to file, what evidence to gather, etc.), the proceedings at DOLE/NLRC could be smoother. This tool can serve as a triage system – straightforward cases (like small wage claims) might even be resolved through the information given (the worker negotiates after learning their rights), whereas more complex ones will be funneled to formal mechanisms. By reducing the load of basic inquiries on government offices (imagine fewer people lining up at DOLE just to ask “Am I entitled to overtime pay?” because they got that answer online), resources could be better spent on the cases that truly need human attention[95].
However, empowerment through information is just one piece. For sustainable impact, awareness of the chatbot is key. This raises a challenge of dissemination: partnering with labor unions, worker associations, or government programs would likely be necessary to reach those who need it. One can envision DOLE itself adopting or endorsing such a chatbot on their website or Facebook page. If integrated into official channels, it gains instant credibility and a wide user base. There is precedent – the Supreme Court’s free legal help chatbots in other countries succeeded when hosted on official websites[4]. A similar approach here, e.g., a DOLE chatbot, could institutionalize the innovation. We have to consider, though, that government adoption would require thorough vetting and perhaps more conservative behavior from the AI. Our results provide a proof-of-concept that can be presented to such stakeholders.
Ethical Considerations and Risks
Deploying an AI in the legal domain carries ethical responsibilities. One primary concern is the risk of a “two-tier” justice system[96] – where the poor get AI help while the rich get lawyers. Simshaw (2022) warns of this inequity[97]. Our view is that in contexts like the Philippines, the alternative for most poor workers is nothing at all, not a lawyer. So an AI assistant is a clear improvement in accessibility. Nevertheless, to avoid complacency, the chatbot should be part of a broader ecosystem that still strives to increase availability of human legal aid. Ideally, it can augment human efforts: for example, paralegals or volunteer lawyers might use the chatbot to handle basic questions, freeing them for complex tasks. In our engagement plan, we consider working with NGOs so that the chatbot can escalate certain queries to a human if needed (particularly sensitive cases like illegal dismissal of a union officer, which might be strategic litigation).
Unauthorized practice of law (UPL) remains a legal grey area. The chatbot explicitly disclaims that it’s not a lawyer, but we should also ensure it doesn’t masquerade as one. In user studies, a few still said they’d probably double-check with a lawyer – this skepticism is actually healthy. We don’t want users to blindly trust an AI for life-altering decisions. Hence, maintaining a certain level of caution in the user is desirable. Our design fosters this by providing sources and using conditional language (“you may do X, if Y applies”). Also, by encouraging follow-up questions, we allow users to probe the advice, which can reveal any uncertainties.
Another ethical aspect is bias. We tried to ensure neutrality, but one might ask: the Labor Code is generally pro-worker; does that mean the chatbot inherently favors employees? It will, by design, inform workers of their rights, which could be seen as “biased” towards labor. We argue this is simply reflecting the law – which indeed provides workers with specific protections. If an employer uses it, they get the same information (which might tell them what they are obliged to do legally). So in effect, it isn’t biased, it’s just following the protective stance of labor laws. The bias testing we did (male vs female scenarios, etc.) did not show disparity in treatment[98][56]. However, one must remain vigilant that the AI doesn’t inadvertently use derogatory or culturally insensitive language. Our inclusion of polite particles (“po/opo”) and culturally aware tone is meant to show respect, especially given many users might be older or in distress[99][100]. We would also look out for any pattern like the bot being more encouraging to one gender – but since it doesn’t identify the user’s gender unless told, this was not an issue.
Privacy is another risk. We have a clear policy of not storing data and not sharing it[101]. But when integrated into a platform (say Facebook Messenger), users must also trust that platform’s privacy. End-to-end, if we deploy on Messenger like Tisya Hustisya did, we have to abide by Facebook’s data rules and reassure users about them. Another idea is deploying via SMS or offline means for those with no internet, but SMS isn’t secure or rich enough for this kind of interaction. Likely a smartphone app or web app is the vehicle, which inherently means some data flows. We considered if sensitive cases (like sexual harassment) raise obligations for the bot – currently, it will not report anything, which respects confidentiality, but we might need disclaimers about if the user shares info about crimes (just as counselors are sometimes mandated reporters for certain things). In labor context, that’s less likely except maybe child labor cases (sharing that might ethically oblige contacting authorities). This has to be thought through with legal ethicists.
Lastly, there is the risk of over-reliance on AI. If such chatbots become common, might institutions start expecting individuals to use them and cut back further on human services? For instance, a government might reduce funding for labor inspectors if “the chatbot is telling workers their rights.” That would be a perverse outcome. We see the chatbot as a complement, not a replacement. It can guide and inform, but it cannot negotiate settlements, make judgments, or enforce laws – human roles remain indispensable. The best scenario is a hybrid: the chatbot empowers more people to step forward, which then encourages more robust funding of labor dispute resolution because now more cases come to light. To ensure this positive feedback, we suggest continued involvement of human legal aid organizations in the loop. Possibly, the chatbot could have a feature (with user consent) to forward a summary of the issue to a volunteer lawyer network for follow-up.
Comparison with Existing Solutions
Our domain-specific chatbot can be contrasted with both traditional and AI-based solutions. Compared to traditional legal aid clinics or PAO services, the chatbot offers instant responses and wide availability but lacks the personalized, empathetic touch of a human lawyer who can handle complex strategy. It excels at providing information and procedural guidance (which often is all someone needs to take initial action), but it cannot represent someone in a court or truly negotiate on their behalf. That said, many issues never reach court – for those, information was the main barrier, so the chatbot effectively addresses that stage.
Compared to Tisya Hustisya, the scripted Q&A approach, our chatbot demonstrated far more flexibility. Tisya might have had, say, a fixed response for “What is the minimum wage?” coded in. Our chatbot can handle variations of that question, follow-ups like “what if I’m paid weekly, how to compute,” and even multi-part queries. It’s essentially a next-gen Tisya powered by AI. The trade-off is that Tisya’s answers were manually vetted by lawyers (hence guaranteed accurate, but limited in scope), whereas our AI answers, while broadly accurate, carry a small risk of error or need for oversight. However, given our high accuracy rate and continuous learning capability, the AI approach seems justifiable for scaling. Tisya answered 25k queries in 2 years[7]; an AI system could handle that in weeks, not years, once widely used, due to parallel processing and 24/7 availability. We also incorporate the strengths of Tisya – bilingual, rights-affirming language – but extend to more topics within labor.
Other AI chatbots like those in North Carolina or India were more generalist. Having a narrow domain like ours is a point of difference: it allowed us to achieve high accuracy by focusing on one area deeply. The Moroccan RAG chatbot[12], for example, covered multiple legal domains and reported decent performance, but potentially at the cost of depth in any one domain. Our approach can be seen as a template: one could similarly build a chatbot for family law or land disputes separately, each with curated corpora. Users might prefer a one-stop tool though. One idea is a federation of domain bots under a single interface (“Legal Aid Bot” that internally routes to labor-specialist, family-specialist modules etc.). For now, proving the concept in labor law is a significant step.
In terms of user trust and adoption, our study indicates that people are open to using the chatbot and even preferring it for initial questions (some said they’d rather ask the bot than a non-lawyer friend, which currently 72% do[2][102]). This is promising, because it suggests the public is not averse to an AI helper. However, trust has to be continually earned; one bad instance or widely publicized mistake could set things back. Therefore, continual monitoring and improvement are needed (possibly through periodic re-evaluation like we did, or integrating a feedback loop in the chatbot UI where users can flag answers). We imagine deploying initially as a pilot, maybe in partnership with a law school clinic or NGO, to gather more usage data and fine-tune before a full public release.
Limitations
Despite our encouraging results, it’s important to acknowledge the limitations of our work and the system:
•	Scope of Knowledge: By design, we limited the domain to national labor law. This means the chatbot might not handle questions that involve other areas, even if related (e.g., a question about criminal liability of an employer, or a family law issue like support which could intersect with employment if someone asks about maternity benefits and child support in one breath). In testing, off-domain questions were politely refused. This keeps the bot focused but also means a user with a mixed issue might not get a holistic answer. Real-life problems aren’t neatly siloed. For instance, an OFW might ask about employment contract (labor) and immigration status (not labor) together – our bot would drop the latter. This is a limitation in user experience. One solution later is to integrate multiple knowledge bases or have a triage that can pass a question to the right domain model if it detects multi-domain queries. Currently, we caution that the bot is not omniscient; users should be aware of its scope (which is why we have the message “covers national laws; may not account for local ordinances or other areas”[11][54]).
•	Local Variations & Latest Updates: Labor law in the Philippines can have industry-specific practices or local wage orders, etc. Our knowledge base included general laws and the latest national wage orders, but not every local detail. For example, if someone inquires about the minimum wage, the answer might give the national range or ask their region, but we did not encode every regional wage order explicitly. Similarly, if a new amendment passes (say increasing service incentive leaves from 5 to 7 days, hypothetically), the chatbot needs an update. Thus, maintenance is a concern – the legal domain evolves (though core labor laws are relatively stable, regulations and interpretations change). We propose maintaining a changelog and updating the corpus at least annually or when major laws pass (e.g., in 2022 the Work From Home Law was new). The chatbot currently is as current as 2025; without updates, its accuracy will degrade over time.
•	Depth of Reasoning: While GPT-4.1 is powerful, it doesn’t truly “reason” like a lawyer on complex fact patterns. It excels at Q&A and summarizing known rules, but if faced with a very intricate scenario, it might falter or oversimplify. For example, determining if an employee is actually a contractor (a common complex issue in labor) can involve multiple tests (control test, etc.). The chatbot can describe those tests, but applying them to facts is tricky. We did not attempt to have the AI weigh evidence or make factual determinations – that’s beyond its safe capability. So, it provides guidance but ultimately the user must still do some thinking or seek human counsel for borderline cases. This is a limitation inherent in current AI: they provide information, not a guaranteed solution.
•	Conversational Limit: The current prototype handles a moderate multi-turn conversation (as our example went ~6 turns). But very long conversations could lead to context loss or higher risk of errors. The model has a context window limit (we didn’t hit it in normal use, but theoretically ~8k tokens for GPT-4.1). If a user asks an entire story over 20 turns, the earlier parts might drop out. In legal consults, though, usually the initial story and a few clarifications suffice, so this may be fine. Still, we recommend resetting or summarizing if a chat gets too long. We also noticed the model sometimes repeats itself in later turns (“As I mentioned, you can file at DOLE…”). This can be tuned, but it’s not too bad – redundancy in advice can reinforce the message actually.
•	Dependency on External Service: We used OpenAI’s GPT-4.1. Relying on a third-party API has costs and requires internet connectivity. In deployment, one might want a self-hosted model to reduce cost and dependency. However, the current state-of-the-art for Filipino language understanding might not be as good if using an open model. It’s a trade-off. For now, the performance we got is thanks in part to GPT-4.1’s capabilities. If API costs become an issue, one could explore fine-tuning a smaller model on our Q&A data. That could be future work, but it’s not guaranteed to match GPT-4’s quality in nuanced legal responses.
•	User Constraints: Using a chatbot assumes literacy (both in reading and digital). Some workers (especially older or those in very remote areas) may not be comfortable typing out their problems. There’s a risk that the ones most in need (e.g., non-English speaking rural farmers or factory workers with limited education) might not know about or be able to use the chatbot effectively. We tried to mitigate this by making it Filipino-friendly and potentially accessible via mobile. If voice input or voice output could be added, that might broaden accessibility further (e.g., someone could talk to it over a phone call with speech-to-text conversion). But that’s beyond our current scope. It’s important to blend tech solutions with community education – e.g., NGOs could hold clinics teaching workers how to use the chatbot.
In sum, while the chatbot shows great promise as a scalable legal aid tool for labor issues, it is not a panacea. It should be deployed with these limitations in mind, serving as an aid rather than a final authority. Continuous improvement, user feedback incorporation, and alignment with human legal support are key to its long-term success.
Conclusion
This paper presented the design and evaluation of a domain-specific AI legal aid chatbot focusing on Philippine labor law – a novel approach aimed at improving access to justice for Filipino workers. By leveraging a Retrieval-Augmented Generation architecture with a state-of-the-art language model, the system is able to engage in bilingual, interactive dialogues with users, providing them with relevant legal information and guidance grounded in the country’s labor code, regulations, and procedures. Our research demonstrated that narrowing the chatbot’s domain to labor law yields significant benefits: high accuracy in answering questions about wages, dismissal, benefits, and other workplace rights, and highly relevant, actionable advice (such as directing users to the DOLE Single Entry Approach for dispute resolution[47]).
Through rigorous evaluation on prototypical labor issues and a pilot user study, we found that the chatbot can correctly address around 88% of queries – a level comparable to human-curated advice resources – and users reported strong satisfaction and understanding from the assistance provided. Notably, the chatbot can operate in English or Filipino seamlessly, thereby breaking the language barrier that often accompanies legal discourse in the Philippines. Users with little prior knowledge of the law were able to learn their rights (e.g., that firing without just cause is unlawful, or that 13th-month pay must be given by December 24) and the steps they can take to seek remedies, all within a few minutes of chatting with the AI. These findings underscore the potential of AI to serve as a scalable proxy for initial legal guidance in a country where lawyers are scarce and legal aid is overburdened[3].
Our work contributes to both the technology and public policy discussions. On the technical side, we showed that a careful curation of legal texts and example-driven prompt engineering can enable a large language model to function as a useful legal assistant in a low-resource language environment. The use of retrieval helped ground the model’s outputs in actual laws, mitigating the risk of hallucination and increasing trust via citations[10]. We also highlighted the importance of conversation design elements like clarifying questions and culturally aware language usage in making the interaction effective and user-friendly. These techniques can be generalized to other domains – for instance, a similar approach could be tried for family law or agrarian law in the Philippines, by building the respective knowledge bases and taxonomies.
On the socio-legal side, our project offers a case study in how AI might be responsibly deployed to augment the justice system. We incorporated ethical safeguards such as disclaimers to avoid misrepresentation as a lawyer, refusal of inappropriate queries, and a feedback loop for oversight. We envision that a system like this could be implemented in collaboration with government agencies (e.g., DOLE’s labor communications division or the NLRC) or civil society organizations working on labor rights. By doing so, it could reach a wider audience and be regularly updated by domain experts. Ultimately, a labor law chatbot is not a replacement for legal professionals but a force multiplier – handling routine inquiries and empowering individuals with information, while flagging complex cases for human attention. If scaled, such an AI tool could help balance the scales for many workers who currently have no practical means to know or assert their lawful rights, thereby contributing to a more equitable labor justice landscape.
Future Work: There are several avenues to extend this research. One immediate next step is deploying the chatbot in a real-world pilot and studying usage patterns and outcomes. For example, partnering with a DOLE regional office to offer the chatbot to walk-in complainants as a “first help desk” could provide valuable insights (and measure if it reduces processing times or increases correct filings). We also plan to broaden language support – exploring integration of a Cebuano language model or a translation layer so that Visayan-speaking users can interact in their mother tongue, since the Visayas and Mindanao regions have large labor populations. Another technical extension is integrating a small-case reasoning ability, possibly by training the model on annotated scenarios, to handle multi-issue queries better or do simple legal reasoning (like computing total claims for unpaid wages + separation pay). Additionally, we aim to incorporate a referral system: e.g., after the chatbot has given advice, it could, with user consent, log a summary that could be forwarded to a local legal aid clinic or DOLE officer who can follow up, thereby creating a bridge between AI and human services.
Finally, we look to adapt the approach to other domains of public interest law in the Philippines. The success in labor law suggests that similar RAG-powered chatbots could be developed for areas such as family law (for questions on annulment, child support, etc.) or community/land disputes – essentially creating a suite of AI legal aids specialized per field. These could live under one umbrella platform, guiding citizens through various common legal problems. Each domain will pose its own challenges, but the lessons learned from the labor law chatbot – especially regarding language, cultural context, and integration with legal processes – will be invaluable in those efforts.
In conclusion, the development of a Philippine labor law chatbot illustrates a promising fusion of AI technology with public service. It shows that with thoughtful design and domain expertise, AI can help democratize legal knowledge, enabling ordinary people to navigate the complexities of law. While limitations and risks must be managed, the opportunity to narrow the justice gap for workers and other disadvantaged groups is a compelling reward. We hope this work sparks further collaborations between technologists, legal experts, and government in the pursuit of innovative solutions for access to justice in the digital age.
